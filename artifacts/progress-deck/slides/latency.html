Processing is not modelled: it is the same in every scenario. Polar totals: Wooster et al. (2021); ESA.

The detection times of the public-record slide are observation times; users could act only once the product was published. Geostationary satellites are always in contact with the ground, so their products appear within minutes: measured from the downloaded files, GOES products are created 3 minutes after the scan start (5,179 files, maximum 10 minutes) and LSA SAF products 17.5 minutes after the slot start, which includes the 15-minute scan (864 files, maximum 21 minutes). Polar satellites first wait for a ground station: median 25 minutes, up to 114 minutes on the real ground segments. Then the data are processed. The literature gives the total from observation to product: FIRMS usually within 2.5 hours (Wooster et al. 2021, Remote Sensing of Environment), Sentinel-3 under 3 hours (ESA mission requirement). Processing is not modelled: as long as it happens on the ground it is the same in every scenario, so it shifts all results equally. The scenarios are compared on two simulated times: time to detection (validated against S0) and time to ground. Conclusion: after the detection, the ground and processing chain, not the observation, sets the delay. Where a polar satellite sees the fire first (Molezuelas, Oimbra), the public alert can come hours later. S2 shortens the time to ground; the processing after it is the same in every scenario unless it moves on board or to the central nodes.
